Ancestory

by Hannah Salyer

Purpose for Reading

Read to learn about ancient works of art and the stories they tell.

  1. 1:On sun-soaked stone and dark cave walls, from the rocky deserts of the United States . . .
  2. 2:to the lush rainforests of Indonesia . . . time capsules wait to be rediscovered.
  3. 3:These treasures teem with colorful stories about our past. Through them, we are transported and can find our place in the . . .

  4. 4:Ancestory.

    Ancestory: a made-up word that combines the words ancestor and story.

  5. 5:These time capsules take the shape of ancient rock paintings, drawings, and etchings.
  6. 6:But who made them?
  7. 7:Our Ancestors.

  8. 8:Homo sapiens, our own species. Neanderthals too. Their art can be found all over the world.

    Neanderthals: a species related to humans who lived between about 130,000 and 40,000 years ago.

  9. 9:Some of the earliest known rock art was created in the Paleolithic period, long before our ancestors built houses, farms, or cities together.

  10. 10:Though like houses, these works of art took a long time to make. Sometimes they took many people too. And sometimes thousands of years would pass before someone made the next addition.
  11. 11:Each handprint, marking, color, and material had a purpose.
  12. 12:It took an enormous effort to make each piece.
  13. 13:Our ancestors did not have paint or brushes or pencils or ink. They had to rely on minerals from their surroundings instead.
  14. 14:They had to create their own handmade tools to etch into and draw on the stone.
  15. 15:Some of the markings and creatures shown in the art are symbols. These pictures represent something real or imagined and can work together to tell a longer story, like letters in an alphabet.

  16. 16:There are also numerous animals shown in these pieces who are now long extinct.

    An ancient Aboriginal rock art image of a Tasmanian tiger in Kakadu National Park, Australia

  17. 17:The lives of our ancestors were filled with difficulties and danger. They worked hard to survive. And yet, for thousands of years and despite their struggles . . .
  18. 18:They took the time to create.
  19. 19:The planet has changed in countless ways since these works were made. Oceans and ice caps have expanded and then disappeared.
  20. 20:What was once green and blue has turned dry and dusty.
  21. 21:However, these ancient artworks remain—distant memories in stone of what came before us.
  22. 22:Some have been preserved in caves. Others have weathered the elements outdoors.
  23. 23:Today they are incredibly fragile and can be lost in the blink of an eye if we are not careful to protect them.

Cueva de las Manos (Cave of the Hands) in Argentina

  1. 24:When it gets dark, these pictures reveal more of their secrets and their power. They come alive in light and shadow.
  2. 25:Our ancestors used natural shapes in the stone to help define the creatures, so that they seem to emerge from the walls. They were drawn by the light of the setting sun or a flickering flame.
  3. 26:In those glimmers, the figures dance, leap, and run.
  4. 27:Many of the sites also have unique soundscapes: Deep, resonant chambers, rocks that ring when they are hit, and walls that seem to talk back.
  5. 28:What did our ancestors think when they heard these echoes?
  6. 29:Could the art they made be mapping the stars? Documenting the world around them?
  7. 30:Were the sites gateways to connect with spaces and spirits invisible to the naked eye?

  8. 31:We don’t have every clue that we need to know exactly why our ancestors created these pictures or the details of the stories they were telling. Through hard work, though, bits of evidence continue to be found. Archaeologists study tirelessly to connect the dots, and sometimes people who are part of local indigenous communities still have distant familiarity with these ancient sites and stories.

    indigenous: related to people who lived in a place first

  9. 32:One thing is clear: The locations where our ancestors made their art were very important.

Petroglyphs at Signal Hill in Saguarao National Park in Arizona, U.S.A., created by Hohokam people who lived in Arizona from 450 to 1450 CE

  1. 33:They are still important to us because these places hold pieces of our history on the planet. They are our human heritage.
  2. 34:If we step back, these fragments reveal a picture of a larger, ongoing story.
  3. 35:Our Ancestory.
  4. 36:The outlines of this shared story are old, but they are still being drawn. Even now, the story is unfurling all around you.

Make Inferences

An inference is an idea you form based on text evidence and what you already know about the topic or situation. Making inferences helps readers understand ideas that the author does not state directly.

Answer each question by making an inference about ancient art. Start by reading the text evidence. Then think about what you already know about the subject.

  1. 1. What were some challenges our ancestors overcame to make art?

  2. 2. Why do you think our ancestors made art despite all the challenges?

How Words Work

Figurative Language

Figurative language uses words and phrases that mean something different from their literal, or exact, meaning. One type of figurative language is an idiom. An idiom is a common saying that means something different from the usual meaning of the individual words.

Archaeologists look for evidence and try to connect the dots to understand the ancient world.
