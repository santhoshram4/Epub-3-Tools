1.1 Introduction to Plant Biology

Learning Outcomes

  1. List some questions addressed by studies of plants.
  2. Describe potential effects of global climate change on living organisms.

Plant life constitutes more than 98% of the total biomass of the earth. Plants and other green organisms are exclusively equipped to produce oxygen while packing the sun’s energy into compounds needed for life. Conveniently, the backbone of these compounds is carbon, captured by plants from carbon dioxide given off by all living organisms. All life on the earth depends on green organisms. If some major disease killed all green organisms, then all animals would soon starve. Even if some alternative source of energy were available, animal life would suffocate within 11 years—the time estimated for all the earth’s oxygen to be completely used up if it were not replaced. How can plants build leaves and wood and roots from an invisible gas? Why do plants produce oxygen as a by-product?

In addition to providing 90% of the world’s food, plants provide important narcotic and medicinal drugs. Of course, these drugs save lives and improve the quality of life for millions of people. However, some are addictive and can lead to serious challenges for societies and individuals. In 2017, the Department of Health and Human Services declared the opioid epidemic to be a public health emergency. Some opioids are produced directly by plants (Fig. 1.1), while others, such as fentanyl, are synthesized in labs. In 2023, more than 107,000 Americans died due to drug overdose; nearly 70% of those deaths were caused by opioids. Why do plants produce both beneficial and harmful compounds? What are scientists doing to discover and harness the incredible diversity of compounds produced by plants?

We think of animals as consumers of plants, but not the other way around. Do plants ever eat animals? Yes, carnivorous plants consume many types of small animals, including insects, frogs, rodents, and birds (Fig. 1.2). These plants are typically found in bogs, where minerals are difficult to obtain. The animals are important sources of nutrients but not calories. How have plants adapted to grow in extreme environments? What are the limits and costs of these adaptations? Will we be able to tap into plant genetic resources that will allow us to grow crops in marginal environments?

California redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) and giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum) trees can grow to heights of 90 meters (300 feet) or more (Fig. 1.3). Water is heavy and must be carried from the ground to a height equivalent to the length of a football field. This is a very expensive activity, yet these trees lift gallons of water high into the canopy every day and still have enough energy for growth and maintenance. The trees actually expend little energy for water transport. Instead, they take advantage of energy from the sun to pull water from the roots to the leaves. How do plants harness energy from the sun to both build biomass and transport water?

This book addresses these questions and many others about living organisms. In addition to plants, we will investigate the lives and impacts of a diverse array of life-forms, including bacteria, fungi, and protists, including algae. The journey is constantly changing as our understanding of the natural world expands. Use this book as a solid resource, but continue to explore, observe, and learn beyond its pages.

KEY THEME 1.1 ecology

Who Needs Plants?

H

uman existence depends on plants. In fact, we need plants more than we need animals. In addition to providing oxygen essential to all aerobic organisms, plants act as the foundations of our civilizations, providing food, animal feed, shelter, clothing, industrial products, and medicines. Despite our dependence on plants, though, human activities threaten their survival. Given the fundamental need for plants by humans, it is surprising that conservation efforts in past decades have instead focused on so-called charismatic megafauna (large mammals such as tigers and whales). In recent years, though, there has been a refocusing of conservation strategies to be more inclusive of all members of the biotic world, including plants, fungi, bacteria, and algae. This new direction reflects the growing recognition that preservation of all biological diversity is important for the survival of the human race.
